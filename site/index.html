    About

    About and methods

    How the comparison works, what it can and can't tell you, and where everything comes from.

    The data

    Two forecasts from NOAA, the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration: its AI model (AIGFS, version 1.1) and its physics model (GFS). Both forecast the temperature 2 meters above the ground, and we read each at the grid point nearest six weather stations. The measurements come from those stations' own reports. A nightly job saves each evening, because NOAA keeps the AI forecasts for only about 10 days.

    How a case is compared

    A case is one place, one evening and one forecast length: 3, 5 or 7 days ahead. Each evening is 00:00 UTC, about 7 p.m. in New York and 4 p.m. in Seattle. A forecast's miss is how far it was from the station's reading. Forecasts are shown in whole degrees, because they are estimates; misses and averages keep one decimal.

    How we call a winner

    We only say a model did better when its average advantage is more than twice its standard error, a common rule of thumb for "bigger than the day-to-day ups and downs". Otherwise we say it's too close to call. It's a rough guide: cases on the same evening share the same weather, so they aren't fully independent. Counts always come with their share, such as "31 of 96 (32%)".

    Rain

    Since 19 November 2026 the archive also saves rain: each model's total for the 6 hours ending at 00:00 UTC, and what each station measured in the same 6 hours. A station that reports no rain while its rain gauge works counts as dry; a broken or missing report counts as missing, never as dry. "Rain" means at least 0.01 inch, the National Weather Service's measurable amount. Rain will appear on this page once enough evenings are saved.

    What this can't tell you

    Six stations and a few weeks of evenings are a small sample, and the findings will change as the record grows. A grid point can miss what makes a place special, such as a city park or a valley. This is a learning tool, not a forecast: for real forecasts and warnings, go to weather.gov. See also what the AI model can't do yet.

    Updates

    A new evening is added each night. If a source is down, the job saves what it can and tries again on later nights; a reading it can't get is shown as missing. The footer says how fresh the data is.

    Accessibility

    We aim for WCAG 2.2 level AA. The page has been checked in light and dark themes, at narrow phone widths, with text enlarged to 200%, with the keyboard alone and with VoiceOver, and with real people. Charts have spoken descriptions and tables beside them, and color is never the only cue. Not yet checked: Windows high-contrast mode. If something doesn't work for you, please tell us.

    Privacy

    No accounts, no cookies, no tracking, and nothing loaded from other sites. Your theme and unit choices are kept in your own browser so the page remembers them; nothing you choose or type is sent anywhere.

    Glossary

    Words used here

    Forecast
    An estimate of what the weather will do. It can be a few degrees off, so this page rounds forecasts to whole degrees.
    Model
    A computer program that works out what the weather will do next.
    AI model (AIGFS)
    NOAA's model that learned from years of past weather. It runs on a tiny fraction of the computing the physics model needs.
    Physics model (GFS)
    NOAA's model built from the laws of motion, heat and moisture.
    Miss
    How far a forecast was from what the thermometer measured.
    Running cold
    Forecasting lower temperatures than really happened, again and again. Scientists call a lean like this a bias.
    Rule
    What you decide to do with the forecasts, such as "act only if both models agree".
    False alarm
    You acted, and the weather didn't come.
    Caught off guard
    The weather came, and you hadn't acted.

Teaching With Two Forecasts, One Sky

A lesson plan for 30 to 45 minutes. Learners compare two weather forecasts with what really happened, then decide how they'd act on them.

Free to use and adapt (CC BY 4.0)
At a glance

At a glance

Time
30 to 45 minutes. The activity alone takes about 10.
Who
Middle school to adult. Works for science, math, media literacy and AI literacy.
Set-up
One shared screen, or pairs on devices. Paper for notes.
Accounts
None. Nothing learners choose is saved or sent.

By the end, learners can

  1. Compare an AI forecast and a physics forecast with what a weather station measured.
  2. Explain why one case, or one evening, isn't enough to judge a forecast.
  3. Choose a rule for acting on forecasts, and say what it trades: false alarms against being caught off guard.

Before class

  • Open the activity on the screen you'll use, and check that it loads.
  • Choose one case to start with. New York on 4 November, 5 days ahead, works well: the AI model ran cold there.
  • Say up front: this is a learning tool, not a forecast. Real forecasts and warnings are at weather.gov.
  • The numbers grow each night, so your class may see different results from this plan. Nothing in the plan depends on them.
The lesson

The lesson, step by step

  1. 1 Open with a question 5 minutes

    Ask: "Would you trust a forecast made by AI, or one worked out from physics? Why?" Take a show of hands. Don't settle it. Say they'll check it against real measurements.

  2. 2 One case together 5 minutes

    On the shared screen, do steps 01 to 03 with your chosen case. Everyone writes a guess before you choose Show what happened. Ask: "Did your guess match? Does one case tell us which model is better?"

  3. 3 Pairs try more cases 10 minutes

    Each pair takes a different place. They guess first, then look, three times, and note the results on paper. Then open The bigger picture together. Ask: "Why might one case mislead us? What does the evening-by-evening chart show?"

  4. 4 Make a decision 10 to 15 minutes

    Give each group a role from step 04: emergency manager, farmer, teacher or event organizer. Every group uses the same weather and lead time, and picks its own rule. Compare: who had more false alarms, and who was caught off guard more? Which mattered more for their role?

  5. 5 Close 5 minutes

    Read the Before you go question aloud: "Think of a forecast you lean on. What would you want to know about it before you trust it more, or less?" Each learner writes one sentence on paper as they leave.

Watch for

What to listen for

Common ideas, and a question that helps.

"The AI won that case, so AI is better."
Ask: "How often did it come closer across all the cases?" Then show the bigger picture.
"The AI model is just worse."
Ask: "Is that true at every distance? And for rain?" Some gaps are too close to call, and rain can tell a different story from temperature.
"A forecast that missed was wrong."
Ask: "How far off would be close enough for a picnic? For a frost warning?" Forecasts are estimates, and how close is close enough depends on the decision.
"These results are final."
Ask: "What might change as more evenings are saved?" A new evening is added each night.
Going further

Going further

Discussion questions

  • Why might an AI model and a physics model make different mistakes?
  • The AI model works on a grid of points about 17 miles apart. What could it miss where you live?
  • Who should decide which rule an emergency manager uses: the forecaster, the manager, or the public?
  • NOAA says its new AI model improved. How would you check a claim like that?

Access and care

  • Everything works with a keyboard and a screen reader. Every chart has a table with the same numbers.
  • Learners can switch to °C, and enlarge the text, without anything breaking.
  • No scores, streaks or timers. Learners can change any answer at any time.
  • If the weather is frightening for someone, for example after a storm, let them take the farmer or teacher role, and keep the focus on how forecasts are made.

About the tool

Two Forecasts, One Sky is a prototype by David Petry. It uses public data from NOAA's National Weather Service and isn't affiliated with or endorsed by NOAA. The About and methods section explains the data and its limits. To report a problem or suggest an idea, open an issue on GitHub.
